A Beginner's Guide to Aspect

How Language Views Time: A Comprehensive Study Guide (Based on C. W. Kreidler, Chapter 10)

Orientation and Overview

When we talk about the world, we do not just report what happened—we choose how to look at it. Consider three ways of describing the same activity in past time: 'I read a novel last summer' presents the reading as a whole completed event; 'I was reading a novel when the phone rang' drops us right into the middle of an ongoing, temporary activity; and 'I have read that novel' looks back at past reading from the perspective of right now. In linguistic semantics, this way of viewing the temporal contour or viewpoint of an event is called aspect.

Key Distinction: Tense vs. Aspect
Tense locates an event on a calendar timeline relative to the moment of speaking (past, present, or future). Aspect describes the internal time shape of the event itself—whether the speaker views it as just beginning, ongoing, repeating over and over, completed in a split second, stretching out over time, or aiming toward a finished goal.

As Charles W. Kreidler explains in Chapter 10 of Introducing English Semantics, aspect is expressed in two complementary ways: (1) Grammatically through verb inflections and auxiliary constructions (like the progressive be + -ing or the perfect have + past participle); and (2) Lexically through the inherent semantic features built directly into different verbs and adjectives (often designated by the German linguistic term Aktionsart, meaning 'kind of action'). For instance, flash inherently describes a momentary flash of time, whereas wait inherently describes an enduring state.

1. The Boundaries of Time: Generic versus Specific Predications

Just as nouns can refer to a whole species (Dogs are loyal) or a specific animal (That dog is barking), sentences can describe situations as timeless general truths or as temporary, bounded occurrences. Kreidler contrasts two fundamental groups of sentences:

  • Generic Statements ('Eternal Truths'): Two and two make four; Rabbits are rodents; The Atlantic Ocean separates Africa and South America. These report permanent situations without beginning or end; they are unbounded.
  • Specific Statements (Temporary States): Gregory is here; I have a headache; The company manufactures silicon chips; Stella seems happy. These report temporary states that exist at present but are subject to change; they are bounded.

Testing for Boundaries: The Four Aspectual Modifiers

We can empirically prove that specific states have boundaries while generic truths do not by testing them with four special time words: already, still, not yet (or still not), and no longer (or not any more). Added to generic truths, they sound bizarre (*Rabbits are still rodents); but added to temporary states, they fit naturally and mark temporal boundaries:

Modifier Past State Present State Boundary & Meaning Textual Example
Already − (No) + (Yes) Marks the beginning (inception) of a state Gregory is already here.
Still + (Yes) + (Yes) Marks the continuation (state has not ended) I still have a headache.
Not yet − (No) − (No) Marks the non-beginning (state hasn't started) The company does not yet manufacture chips.
No longer + (Yes) − (No) Marks the ending (termination) of a state Stella no longer seems happy.

2. Energy and Duration: Stative versus Dynamic Predicates

To analyze verbal meaning, semantics divides all predicates into two master classes: stative predicates and dynamic predicates. Linguist Bernard Comrie formulated an intuitive physical rule to distinguish them:

  • Stative Predicates: Report a state that requires no expenditure of energy to maintain. A state simply continues automatically until an outside force expends energy to alter it (e.g., We waited; The children were hungry; Snow lay on the ground; Ellen needed a dictionary).
  • Dynamic Predicates: Report a situation or activity that requires a continual input of energy to keep going. The moment energy stops being supplied, the activity ceases (e.g., Something moved; The sun came up; The boat drifted along; They discussed the plan).

The Uniformity of States: Homogeneous Parts

Stative predicates are typically durative, meaning they endure over time, answering the question 'How long?' (e.g., waited for an hour, hungry all day). Kreidler emphasizes a crucial distinction between the duration of a state and the duration of an activity: A state consists of homogeneous parts—it is unchanging at every micro-instant. If Jesse had a headache all morning is true, then at every single second from 9:00 to 12:00, Jesse has a headache was true. In contrast, activities are constant but not necessarily continuous at every micro-second: saying Fred and Ethel argued all day or The team practiced from September to November does not mean they were shouting or passing a ball at every single uninterrupted second.

Dynamic Verbs of Gradual Change

Certain dynamic verbs describe a change occurring steadily across a time span: change, deteriorate, dwindle, improve, worsen, mature. In Grandmother's health deteriorated over the next few months, the verb indicates constant cumulative change, even if the rate of change fluctuates.

3. Ticking Clocks and Flashes: Durative versus Punctual Aspect

Actions differ in their temporal span: some extend across time (durative aspect), while others occur in a split-second flash (punctual aspect).

Punctual Verbs and the Iterative Effect

In Kreidler's example, Albert kicked a ball and the ball struck a post, both kick and strike designate momentary actions that take place in a fraction of a second. Other punctual verbs include: flash, hit, jump, leap, knock, slap. When a punctual verb is forced into a duration phrase (e.g., Albert kicked a ball for ten minutes; He knocked on the door for five minutes), our minds cannot stretch the instantaneous act like rubber. Instead, we automatically interpret the act as being repeated over and over—producing an iterative aspect.

Verbs of Mental Activity: Ingressive versus Durative

English verbs of mental activity can shift between split-second entry and ongoing state depending on context:

  • Punctual / Ingressive (The Sudden Inception): I suddenly remembered the appointment; He felt a sharp pain; Suddenly she knew what to do. Expresses entering into a state (ingressive, inchoative, or inceptive aspect).
  • Durative (The Ongoing State): I remembered all along what she had told me; He felt miserable; She knew the answer yesterday. Expresses maintaining an existing state over time.
  • Causative Ingressive: In Harry reminded me of the appointment, Harry acts as an outside agent causing me to enter the state of remembering.

Past Tense Interpretations and Habitual Aspect

A simple past verb like They sat in the last row can be interpreted as: (1) Punctual: The physical act of sitting down (They sat down); (2) Durative: Remaining seated (They were sitting); or (3) Habitual: A customary practice across occasions (They sat in the last row every time they went to the theater). While simple past requires a frequency phrase to express habit, English has a dedicated grammatical marker strictly reserved for customary past habit: used to (They used to sit in the last row). In present time, simple present expresses habitual aspect: He smokes cigarillos, drinks tea, and sleeps eight hours every night.

4. Goals and Endpoints: Telic versus Atelic Predications

In 1967, philosopher Zeno Vendler proposed a landmark four-way classification of predicates: States, Activities, Achievements, and Accomplishments. To understand Vendler's system, we must first master the distinction between telic and atelic:

  • Telic (from Greek telos, 'goal' or 'end'): Describes an event that moves toward an inherent completion point or natural finish line. The event is not realized until that goal is reached.
  • Atelic: Describes an activity or state that has no built-in finish line. It can stop at any arbitrary second without being incomplete.

Vendler's Four Predicate Classes

Kreidler illustrates how Vendler's four classes operate in English:

  1. States (Atelic + Durative + Non-Dynamic): George was waiting; Sandra was holding the baby; The bottle is empty. Require no energy and have no finish line.
  2. Activities (Atelic + Durative + Dynamic): Sandra was swimming; George was running; The children played. Require continuous energy and last across time, but have no inherent goal.
  3. Achievements (Telic + Instantaneous + Dynamic): George left; Sandra died; The plane landed; He awoke; We arrived home. Split-second transitions to a finished goal. We ask 'When did they arrive?' but never '*How long did they arrive?'
  4. Accomplishments (Telic + Durative + Dynamic): Sandra wrote a letter; George cut the rope; The child grew up. Possess an inherent goal, but require a duration of effort to reach completion.

The Interruption Test & Preposition Diagnostic

How do we empirically distinguish Activities from Accomplishments? Semantics uses two reliable tests:

  • The Interruption Test: If Sandra is swimming and gets interrupted after five minutes, did she swim? Yes. With an atelic activity, an interrupted action has still occurred. But if Sandra is writing a letter and gets interrupted halfway through, did she write a letter? No. She engaged in writing (activity), but she did not produce a letter (accomplishment).
  • The Preposition Test (For vs. In): Activities occur throughout an open period marked by for (Lucy wrote for an hour). Accomplishments require a period leading to completion marked by in (Lucy wrote the letter in an hour).

How Noun Phrases and Verb Particles Shift Aspect

A verb's aspect is not fixed in stone; sentence components interact to determine the overall aspect:

  • Unbounded Nouns make sentences Atelic: In Lucy wrote letters for an hour, the indefinite plural noun letters is open-ended, creating an atelic activity. But in Lucy wrote the letter / three letters in an hour, the bounded noun phrase creates a telic accomplishment.
  • Particles turn Atelic verbs into Telic verbs: Adding a particle like up, down, or out creates a completion goal: The papers burned (atelic activity) versus The papers burned up (telic accomplishment).
Predicate Class Dynamic? Durative? Telic? Preposition / Diagnostic Example
State − (No) + (Yes) − (No) Jesse had a headache for an hour.
Activity + (Yes) + (Yes) − (No) Sandra swam for thirty minutes.
Achievement + (Yes) − (No) + (Yes) George arrived at noon. (instantaneous)
Accomplishment + (Yes) + (Yes) + (Yes) Sandra wrote a letter in thirty minutes.

5. Phases of an Event: Ingressive, Continuative, and Egressive Aspect

Every situation or event has a beginning, a middle, and an end. In semantics, these three temporal stages are: (1) Ingressive Aspect (or inchoative / inception): the beginning boundary; (2) Continuative Aspect (or duration): the middle continuation; and (3) Egressive Aspect (or terminative): the ending boundary. Kreidler demonstrates that English systematically organizes these phases across five major semantic domains:

Domain Baseline State Ingressive (Beginning) Continuative (Middle) Egressive (Ending) Causative Counterparts
Location be at, on, in arrive at, reach, get to stay at, remain at leave, depart from put at (start); keep at (stay); take from (end)
Possession have, own get, acquire keep lose, give up give/sell to (start); take/buy from (end)
Cognition know, be aware of learn, find out remember forget teach, inform, tell (cause to know)
Events be at, take place start, begin go on, continue, last stop, end, finish start/begin (start); end/stop (end)
Status / Nouns be a physicist become a physicist remain a bachelor (no single verb) make capital; elect chairman; sharpen

Aspectual Verbs (Aspectualizers)

When expressing the phases of dynamic activities, English uses helper verbs called aspectual verbs (or aspectualizers): begin, commence, start (inception); continue, keep on, go on (continuation); stop, cease, finish, end, leave off (termination). Kreidler highlights two crucial syntactic-semantic contrasts:

  • Stop to eat vs. Stop eating: He stopped eating terminates the activity of eating (an aspectual verb). He stopped to eat pauses an outside action in order to eat (an infinitive of purpose, not an aspectual verb).
  • Keep vs. Keep from: Keep the flag flying expresses continuative aspect; Keep the flag from flying expresses prevention (causing non-occurrence).

Presuppositions and Implications of Phase Verbs (Table 10.4)

What do aspectual verbs truly mean? Kreidler formalizes their underlying logic in Table 10.4: Every aspectual verb carries a presupposition about the state of affairs before the event, and an implication about the state afterwards. Notice that affirmative and negative statements share the exact same presupposition:

Aspectual Phase Positive Before Positive After Negative Before Negative After Semantic Truth Meaning
Initiation (start) − (No) + (Yes) − (No) − (No) A negative state existed before; changes to positive after.
Continuation (keep) + (Yes) + (Yes) + (Yes) − (No) A positive state existed before; remains positive after (zero change).
Termination (stop) + (Yes) − (No) + (Yes) + (Yes) A positive state existed before; changes to negative after.

6. Looking Forward and Looking Back: Prospective and Retrospective Meanings

Human thought constantly plans for the future and reflects on the past. In semantics, this forward- and backward-looking orientation is called prospective and retrospective aspect.

  • Full Clauses vs. Reduced Clauses: A full clause with that allows any tense (Fred figured his friends had started / were starting / would start). But a reduced clause (infinitive or gerund) is locked into the perspective of the main verb:
  • Prospective Verbs (Forward-Looking): Oriented toward subsequent behavior, taking an infinitive (aim, intend, plan, prepare, attempt, try, want, promise). Example: We asked Ronald to drive slower (the driving follows the asking).
  • Retrospective Verbs (Backward-Looking): Oriented toward prior behavior, taking a gerund (regret, resent, admit, confess, apologize for). Example: Edgar apologized for missing the meeting (the missing preceded the apology).
  • Preposition Perspective Flip: In Mr. Hawkins excused Jerry for missing the meeting (retrospective), the excuse follows the absence. In Mr. Hawkins excused Jerry from attending the meeting (prospective), the excuse precedes the meeting, granting future exemption.

Grammatical Prospective and Retrospective

  1. The Grammatical Prospective (be + to + verb): Expresses official arrangements or future plans: They are to leave tomorrow; You are not to worry.
  2. The Present Retrospective (The Present Perfect: have + past participle): Rather than 'present perfect', Kreidler terms this the present retrospective because it connects past events to present relevance: (a) Measuring duration from the past: The house has been empty for a year; (b) Past experience relevant now: I have been here three times this month.

7. Zooming In on the Moment: The Progressive (Bounded Aspect)

The English progressive (be + -ing) is traditionally called 'continuous' or 'ongoing.' Kreidler argues that a far more accurate semantic name is bounded aspect (or temporary aspect).

Unbounded Habit versus Bounded Moment

Compare the simple present with the progressive:

  • Jack smokes a pipe: Unbounded, habitual, and generic. It describes Jack's general custom in life, without implying a start or finish.
  • Jack is smoking a pipe: Bounded, temporary, and specific. It zooms in on the immediate present moment, implying the activity will come to an end.

You can say: Jack smokes a pipe, but he isn't smoking it now; but you cannot say: *Jack is smoking a pipe, but he doesn't smoke. The bounded progressive is a temporary subset of the general habit.

Frank Vlach's Empty Theater Seat Puzzle
Linguist Frank Vlach observed that you can point to an empty theater seat with a coat on it and ask: 'Is anyone sitting here?' If the progressive required 100% continuous physical occupation at the split-second of asking, the question would be absurd! It actually means: 'Has this seat been claimed by someone within a bounded period that includes the present?' The progressive expresses distribution over a bounded time frame, not unbroken micro-second continuity.

Progressive with Achievements and Statives

  • Achievements in Progressive Focus on Incompleteness: Momentary verbs like leave or land cannot literally be in progress. Saying Our guests are leaving or The plane is landing emphasizes that the act is not yet complete.
  • Adding Emotional Drama and Intensity: Progressive forms add personal warmth and immediacy: We admire your house (neutral state) vs. We're admiring your house! (lively appreciation); They arrive tomorrow (dry schedule) vs. They're arriving tomorrow! (excited anticipation).

9. Comprehensive Explanatory Answer Key

Review these detailed explanations grounded directly in Charles W. Kreidler's text.

  1. Tense Locates on a Timeline; Aspect Views Internal Shape. Tense locates an event in the past, present, or future relative to the speech moment. Aspect describes the internal temporal structure or viewpoint of the event—whether it is beginning, ongoing, completed, instantaneous, repeating, or aiming at a goal.
  2. Temporary States vs. Eternal Truths. Stella is here describes a bounded temporary state that has a beginning and an end, so the aspectual modifier already can highlight that inception has occurred. Rabbits are rodents is an unbounded generic eternal truth that does not start or stop, making boundary modifiers semantically anomalous.
  3. Continuous Energy Input. A stative predicate describes a state that requires no expenditure of energy and continues automatically until outside energy changes it. A dynamic predicate describes an event or activity that requires a constant input of energy, stopping the moment energy ceases.
  4. Uniformity at Every Instant. If Jesse had a headache all morning, the statement Jesse has a headache was true at every single micro-second; it consists of completely identical, homogeneous moments. An activity like practicing basketball consists of varied movements, stops, and breathers; it is constant across the season, but not continuous at every split-second.
  5. Repetition (Iterative Aspect). A punctual verb denotes a split-second act with no internal duration. When combined with a duration phrase (for five minutes), the human mind cannot stretch the momentary act like rubber, so it automatically interprets the act as repeating over and over (iterative aspect).
  6. States, Activities, Achievements, Accomplishments. The four classes are States, Activities, Achievements, and Accomplishments. States and Activities are atelic (no inherent goal); Achievements and Accomplishments are telic (possess an inherent completion goal).
  7. Interrupted Atelic vs. Interrupted Telic. Yes, if interrupted while swimming, you still swam (swimming is an atelic activity with no finish line). No, if interrupted halfway through drawing a circle, you did not draw a circle (drawing a circle is a telic accomplishment requiring completion). This proves that telic events require reaching the finish line to be true.
  8. Learn, Remember, Forget. In cognition: learn (or find out) is ingressive (entering into knowing); remember is continuative (maintaining the knowledge); and forget is egressive (terminating the knowledge).
  9. Retrospective (for) vs. Prospective (from). Excused for missing is retrospective; the excuse takes place after the absence occurred. Excused from attending is prospective; the excuse takes place before the meeting, looking forward to future non-attendance.
  10. Generic Habit vs. Temporary Bounded Moment. Jack smokes a pipe is unbounded and habitual, describing Jack's general custom without implying a start or finish. Jack is smoking a pipe is bounded and specific, describing an ongoing activity at the present moment that will soon come to an end. Kreidler calls it 'bounded' because it sets temporary limits on the event.

10. Thinking Deeper: 5 Advanced Discussion Questions

  1. The Camera Lens of the Mind: Why is aspect often described as a 'camera lens' while tense is described as a 'calendar'? How does a speaker's choice of aspect allow them to manipulate what a listener visualizes, even when the facts remain identical?
  2. Vendler's Classes in Video Games and Sports: Think of common actions in games (e.g., sprinting, scoring a goal, building a fortress, holding a shield). Classify each into Vendler's four categories. How do game designers use punctual achievements vs. durative accomplishments to reward players?
  3. The Mystery of Statives in the Progressive: School grammars often claim that English stative verbs (like love or understand) can never appear in the progressive (-ing). Yet slogans say 'I'm lovin' it', and people say 'I'm understanding you better now'. Why do speakers do this, and what extra meaning does progressive add?
  4. Aspect and Guilt in Law: In a trial, compare how two witness statements might influence a jury: (A) 'The defendant was pointing a gun at the clerk.' (B) 'The defendant pointed a gun at the clerk.' How does the progressive in (A) manipulate the perception of duration and incompleteness compared to (B)?
  5. Cross-Linguistic Perspectives on Aspect: Kreidler notes that Slavic languages build aspect into separate verb pairs (perfective vs. imperfective), while English uses helper verbs and particles. If a language lacks grammatical aspect markers entirely, how might its speakers express the difference between a sudden flash and an ongoing habit?
